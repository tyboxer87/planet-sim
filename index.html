    The rules of this little universe

    Cosmic curiosity. Grounded in science.

    This is a playable model, not a precision astrophysics simulator. Mass is always in solar masses (M☉), distance in astronomical units (AU), temperature in Kelvin (K). One Sun is 1 M☉; Earth is about 0.000003 M☉.

    Build, move, experiment

    Drag a card into space to create an object, or click a card then click the canvas. Change spawn mass before placing. Drag an existing body to reposition it; its velocity is retained. Auto orbit gives new bodies a roughly circular velocity around the strongest nearby attractor. In Aim velocity mode, drag from the launch point: the arrow sets the new velocity (1 AU of arrow = 5 AU/yr). Press Escape to cancel placement; Space pauses; Delete removes the selected object. Use Fit to find objects that travel offscreen. The object selector and “Place at view center” control provide keyboard access.

    Mergers conserve mass and linear momentum, and combine remaining fuel by mass. Gas-rich mergers can cross the hydrogen-fusion threshold. A pile of rocks does not simply become a hydrogen-burning star. Brown-dwarf boundaries depend on composition and formation; the ~13-Jupiter-mass deuterium boundary is a convenient convention. Massive blue main-sequence stars become giants later; mass alone does not make a blue giant. Real collisions can shed mass, fragment, or disrupt bodies; here they stick.

    Stars have different endings

    Sun-like stars expand into red giants and shed gas, leaving a white dwarf—not a red dwarf. Low-mass red dwarfs live for hundreds of billions to trillions of years, then are expected to become white dwarfs; none has had time to do so yet. The life bar is an approximate fuel clock, with a short giant stage added for stars above 0.5 M☉.

    Stars initially above about 8 M☉ undergo core-collapse supernovae. This model leaves a neutron star for 8–20 M☉ progenitors and a black hole above 20 M☉. These remnant boundaries are schematic: real outcomes depend on core mass, composition, winds, and companions. A remnant neutron star above 2.3 M☉ collapses here; that is an uncertain model choice. Accreting white dwarfs reaching 1.4 M☉ undergo a simplified thermonuclear supernova.

    Ejected material stays in the simulation as massive gas clouds, so total mass is conserved. After a compressed 60 Myr cooling delay, sufficiently massive clouds become protostars; another 5 Myr ignites fusion. This deliberately shortcuts cloud fragmentation, cooling, and collapse. Supernovae do not directly create ready-made stars. No radiated mass loss, stellar winds, relativity, or detailed chemistry is modeled.

    Warm enough for water ≠ guaranteed life

    Planet temperatures combine radiation from all luminous objects: Teq = 278.3 K × [(1 − albedo) Σ(L/L☉)/(distance/AU)²]¼. Rocky worlds assume Earth-like albedo 0.30 and a 33 K greenhouse offset, tapered toward zero below 100 K equilibrium temperature; gas giants have an intrinsic heat floor. These are rough estimates, not climate calculations. Stellar temperatures are surface effective temperatures; a black hole shows its extremely small Hawking temperature, not a hot accretion-disk temperature.

    Below 273 K a rocky world is tagged icy; 273–373 K is water-friendly under an assumed ~1 bar atmosphere. A world with 100 Myr continuously between 273–323 K grows simulated life. Real life also needs chemistry, water, an atmosphere, and time, and its origin is unknown. Above 373 K worlds are hot, but rocks are only tagged molten above 1,400 K; melting temperature depends on composition and pressure. The green ring is an approximate single-star 273–323 K surface-temperature band, not a guarantee of habitability, especially with multiple stars.

    Two clocks, one playground

    Gravity uses G = 4π² in AU³ M☉⁻¹ yr⁻², all-pairs forces, softened at 0.008 AU, and an adaptive leapfrog integrator. Orbital time and stellar time advance separately so you can see both processes. The orbital clock slows if close encounters need extra precision. Radii and collision cross-sections are enlarged for playability, independently of zoom. Dragging, spawning, and removing objects are external interventions.

    The black-hole ending is an arcade rule

    Real black holes attract distant objects just like any other body of the same mass. They do not vacuum up the universe. Here, the optional finale starts when a black hole reaches 40 M☉, spirals all objects inward, then resets to the solar neighborhood. Switch off “Black-hole finale” for ordinary gravity and accretion.
